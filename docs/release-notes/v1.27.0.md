# WiiKart 1.27.0

Real trail-braking technique, finally rewarded by the physics itself.
Engine braking, traction control, and brake lights. A fourth forced-
induction choice. Grip and braking distance both pulled off the list of
things a designer can just dial to whatever they want. Hard tires that
are genuinely the dry-road specialists and genuinely the wrong tire for
anything wet.

## The combined friction circle

A tire only has so much grip, and a real car spends that grip on
whichever of braking, accelerating, or turning it's actually doing —
not a separate, unlimited allowance for each. WiiKart's physics never
modeled that trade-off: a car could brake at its full stopping power
and corner at its full cornering limit at the exact same instant, with
zero interaction between the two.

That's what actually made the classic driving techniques — brake in a
straight line, or trail off the brake progressively as you turn in;
get back on the power early and let the car run out to the wide part
of the exit — pure style rather than something the game noticed or
rewarded. There was no mechanism connecting "how hard you're braking"
to "how much the car can actually turn."

Now there is. Braking (or accelerating) hard while still asking for a
lot of steering angle measurably shrinks how much yaw the car can
actually generate — the same shared tire budget a real car has. Brake
too late, then try to turn in hard while still standing on the pedal,
and the car pushes wide right when you most wanted it to bite. Get the
braking done early, turn in on a trailing throttle, and there's more
grip in reserve exactly when it matters.

This isn't a scripted bonus for driving "correctly" — it's one physics
change, and every AI driver on the grid is subject to it exactly the
same as a human. A driver who brakes late and hard into a corner they're
still turning through will genuinely go slower than one who doesn't,
without a single line of AI decision logic having to change.

## Engine braking and traction control

Lift off the gas and a real engine keeps turning with the wheels
through a closed throttle — compression and pumping losses hold the
car back on their own, more so at higher revs. WiiKart cars used to
just coast on drag and rolling resistance alone the instant the
throttle closed, which felt like piloting a golf cart down a mountain
pass. Now lifting off actually slows the car, and a downshift before a
descent does something real.

A simple traction control system rides alongside it: the instant the
tires read as already sliding under acceleration, engine power gets
trimmed back automatically, easing back in as the slide clears. It
won't save a driver from every mistake, but wheelspin off the line or
out of a slow hairpin no longer compounds itself as hard as it used to.

## Twin-turbo, and forced induction that costs something

A turbo, a supercharger, or now a twin-turbo — the biggest power bonus
of the four, and the heaviest. Every one of them costs curb weight now,
on top of whatever the underlying horsepower figure already costs. A
naturally aspirated engine pays none of it, which finally makes "build
a bigger NA engine instead of bolting on a turbo" a real, competitive
choice rather than a strictly worse one — you get to spend your whole
power-to-weight budget on displacement, nothing held back for hardware
you don't have.

## Grip, braking distance, and what's actually a free dial

Two numbers that used to be free-standing car stats aren't any more.

Grip already derived from mass and drag rather than being an
independent slider, but the drag term ran backwards: more drag area
used to mean more grip, treating it like assumed aerodynamic downforce.
That's a real trade-off for an open-wheel racer with a wing. It's
backwards for the road cars and karts on this roster, where drag is
just blunt frontal area — the same bluntness that costs top speed also
disturbs the air around the contact patches. Less drag now means more
grip. Mass is unchanged: lighter cars are still grippier, which tracks
with tire load sensitivity — real rubber measurably loses peak
coefficient of friction as the load on the contact patch grows.

Braking distance went further: the simulation doesn't even read the
old per-car stopping-distance number any more. Braking is
traction-limited now, off the exact same lateral grip figure everything
else already reads — which happens to make it mass-independent, the
same way real braking deceleration is (the braking force and the
car's inertia both scale with mass and cancel out). There was never a
believable reason a designer should be able to just dial in the
shortest stop physically possible for free; now there's no dial to
turn in the first place. The garage and the designer show the real,
computed number.

## Tires that actually specialize

Hard rubber is now the best tire for straight-line acceleration and
braking on dry pavement — a new number, separate from the existing
cornering grip figure, governs that specifically. It's the trade a
harder, more durable compound genuinely makes in exchange for less
outright stick through a corner.

That dry-road advantage evaporates completely the moment the road
isn't dry. Hard is now the worst tire in snow, on ice, and in standing
water — including the puddle stage, which used to be the one condition
it was actually best in. No tread to fall back on, so it's the one
most likely to skate and fishtail once the payment comes due.

## Brake lights, and a real kickdown

Small things that round the rest out: brake lights now actually light
up when the brake is genuinely being applied (not for engine braking,
and not the instant FLOOR IT has overridden a held brake). And FLOOR IT
itself does more on an automatic gearbox — with nothing already spooled
to lean on, planting the pedal is now a real kickdown, dropping a gear
immediately if a lower one genuinely makes more power at the current
road speed, the way a real automatic's passing gear works.
